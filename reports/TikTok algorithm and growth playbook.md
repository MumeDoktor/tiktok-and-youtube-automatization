# TikTok rewards watch time, punishes reuse

TikTok's For You feed ranks each video separately for each viewer on predicted watch behaviour, and watch time is the only signal that TikTok's own statements, the leaked 2021 "Algo 101" document and independent audits all agree on; almost every number creators quote about it (200 to 500 viewer test batches, 70% completion, the first-hour rule, ranked point values for shares and saves) has no official or audited source. High view counts are a retention problem played as a volume lottery: the median TikTok gets about 500 views at every posting cadence, about 1 post in 35 passes 10,000 and 1 in 358 passes 100,000, and posting more often raises the size of the outliers, not the median. Small accounts lost roughly 11% to 59% of their average views between 2024 and 2025 while large accounts gained, so benchmarks from older guides overstate what to expect. For the two accounts in question the binding constraint is policy, not craft. The nuclear-history channel is on defensible ground: generic text-to-speech narration is explicitly exempt from AI labelling and US-government film is outside US copyright, but its reach depends on reviewers accepting narration and editing as original input. The podcast-clip channel is the weak one: re-captioned third-party clips match TikTok's written definition of unoriginal content, cannot join Creator Rewards on the written rules, and are three rightsholder reports away from a ban. Phone-based UI automation is named in no policy text, but a broadly worded August 2026 clause could be applied to it, and nothing reliable is known about how TikTok detects it. The evidence base is thin in the places that matter most to these accounts: there is no data on niche pivots, mismatched followers, AI voices, same-day double posting, or whether replying to comments lifts reach on TikTok.

## Watch time is the one signal every evidence tier agrees on

The notes behind this report sort claims into three tiers, and the report keeps them apart: what TikTok has published, what leaks, filings and audits show, and what circulates in creator blogs. Two caveats apply throughout. TikTok's current Help Center and Transparency Center pages on recommendations could not be rendered during research, so the canonical official text is still the June 2020 Newsroom post; and the leaked document is from 2021, with no later leak confirming that the formula is unchanged.

### What TikTok has published

TikTok names three signal families: user interactions (likes, shares, follows, comments, content created), video information ("captions, sounds, and hashtags") and device and account settings (language, country, device type). It has given exactly one statement of relative weight: "A strong indicator of interest, such as whether a user finishes watching a longer video from beginning to end, would receive greater weight than a weak indicator, such as whether the video's viewer and creator are both in the same country." It also states that **"neither follower count nor whether the account has had previous high-performing videos are direct factors in the recommendation system"**, and that the feed deliberately diversifies, generally not showing two videos in a row from the same creator or with the same sound ([TikTok Newsroom](https://newsroom.tiktok.com/en-us/how-tiktok-recommends-videos-for-you)). No numeric weights have ever been published. "Not a direct factor" is narrower than "irrelevant": followers can still matter as early viewers whose behaviour the system measures.

Later official material adds features, not ranking rules. Creator Search Insights shows frequently searched topics and "content gap" topics ([TikTok Newsroom](https://newsroom.tiktok.com/creator-search-insights)); TikTok auto-assigns search keywords to videos, which shows it classifies content on its own ([Social Media Today](https://www.socialmediatoday.com/news/tiktok-lets-users-add-relevant-keywords-to-metadata/818507/)); Manage Topics lets viewers turn more than ten topic categories up or down ([TikTok Newsroom](https://newsroom.tiktok.com/personalize-for-you-feed-with-new-features?lang=en-MY)); and since November 2025 the same panel has a slider for how much AI-generated content a viewer sees ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/11/18/tiktok-now-lets-you-choose-how-much-ai-generated-content-you-want-to-see/)). The largest structural change is the US joint venture that closed in January 2026, which "will retrain, test, and update the content recommendation algorithm on U.S. user data" ([TikTok Newsroom](https://newsroom.tiktok.com/announcement-from-the-new-tiktok-usds-joint-venture-llc?lang=en)). No measured before-and-after comparison of US reach exists; creator reports of glitches and zero-view videos in late January 2026 were attributed by Oracle to a data-centre outage ([The Publish Press](https://news.thepublishpress.com/p/something-s-off-with-tiktok)).

### What leaks, filings and audits add

The leaked "TikTok Algo 101" document, whose authenticity a TikTok spokesperson confirmed ([Gizmodo](https://gizmodo.com/leaked-tiktok-doc-reveals-its-obvious-secret-to-an-addi-1848166901)), gives a simplified scoring formula: **"Plike X Vlike + Pcomment X Vcomment + Eplaytime X Vplaytime + Pplay X Vplay"**, adding that "the actual equation in use is much more complicated, but the logic behind is the same." Each candidate video is scored per viewer on predicted likes, comments, expected playtime and whether it gets played; shares, saves and follows do not appear in the simplified version. The document names retention and time spent as the metrics that matter, lists repetition controls such as "same_author_seen" and "same_tag_today", and says "like bait" is identified and suppressed ([Boston.com reprint of NYT](https://www.boston.com/news/commentary/2021/12/06/how-tiktok-reads-your-mind)). Independent tests corroborate the playtime term: Wall Street Journal bots that only lingered on or rewatched videos had their interests identified in under two hours ([Tubefilter](https://tubefilter.com/2021/07/21/tiktok-recommendation-algorithm-wall-street-journal-guillaume-chaslot/)), and a 2025 sock-puppet study found watching has the strongest personalisation effect, growing with longer or repeated views. The same study warns that TikTok audit findings "often hold only in the short term" ([arXiv 2504.18140](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.18140)).

Two further documented facts undercut reverse-engineered "threshold" rules. ByteDance's published Monolith system trains online so the model reacts to feedback in near real time ([arXiv 2209.07663](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2209.07663)), which makes "small exposure first, more as predictions firm up" an emergent behaviour, not a fixed batch. And staff could manually "heat" videos, with an internal document putting heated videos at "around 1-2%" of daily views ([Forbes Australia](https://www.forbes.com.au/news/innovation/tiktoks-secret-heating-button-can-make-anyone-go-viral)), so some viral outliers were never algorithmic.

Ranked lists that put shares above saves above comments above likes, with point values, have no support in any official or leaked source found. The same holds for an account-level "niche authority" score: classification is evidenced at the video and viewer level, and TikTok's statement about prior hits argues against a strong account-level multiplier. Search is a real but small surface, about **0.3% of views** in the one traffic-source dataset available, against **85.1% from the For You feed** and roughly 0.3% from the Following feed ([quso.ai](https://quso.ai/research/tiktok-data-findings)).

## High view counts are a retention problem played as a volume lottery

### Base rates are harsher than most guides admit

Across 32,229 posts tracked for at least 14 days, about **1 in 6 cleared 1,000 views, 1 in 35 cleared 10,000 and 1 in 358 cleared 100,000** ([quso.ai](https://quso.ai/research/tiktok-data-findings)). The platform got harder for small accounts: average views per post fell 59% for 1K to 5K accounts and 40% for 5K to 10K accounts between 2024 and 2025, slipped 11% for 10K to 50K accounts (to about 3,240), and rose 39% for 100K to 1M accounts ([Socialinsider](https://www.socialinsider.io/social-media-benchmarks/tiktok)). A separate study found 72% more videos published year over year and 31% fewer views per video ([Metricool](https://metricool.com/press-release-tiktok-study-2026/)). All of these datasets come from customers of scheduling and clipping tools, which over-represent brands, and all are observational. Applied to a twice-daily account, the population averages imply about two posts a week above 1,000 views, one above 10,000 every two to three weeks, and one above 100,000 roughly twice a year.

### Posting more buys bigger outliers, not a better median

The strongest single piece of evidence in this field is Buffer's fixed-effects study of 11.4 million posts from 150,000 accounts. Against one post a week, views per post rose 17% at 2 to 5 posts, 29% at 6 to 10 and 34% at 11 or more, so frequency does not dilute. But the median barely moves, while the 90th percentile climbs steeply ([Buffer](https://buffer.com/resources/how-often-should-you-post-on-tiktok/)).

| Posts per week | Median views | 90th percentile views |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 489 | 3,722 |
| 2 to 5 | 506 | 6,983 |
| 6 to 10 | 487 | 10,092 |
| 11 or more | 459 | 14,401 |

Two posts a day sits in the top bucket. The study cannot say whether those accounts held quality constant, and nothing isolates whether a second post on the same day cannibalises the first. Timing is a weak lever: Buffer's 7.1 million post analysis favours Sunday and Monday slots and states "There isn't one 'magic' best time to post" ([Buffer](https://buffer.com/resources/best-time-to-post-on-tiktok)), while other large studies favour midweek afternoons or Thursday mornings ([eClincher roundup](https://www.eclincher.com/articles/best-time-to-post-on-tiktok-in-2026-what-the-data-really-says)). Three large studies naming three different windows is itself the finding. The account's own follower-activity chart is the better guide.

### Hooks and retention: the direction is certain, the thresholds are invented

No TikTok-published organic drop-off curve exists, and TikTok does not expose retention curves through its API, so no third party can study them at scale. Every numeric threshold in circulation (65% three-second hold, 70% completion) is anecdote. What is measured is how low typical watch time is: **median 6.9 seconds on 30 to 60 second videos and 11.3 seconds on videos over a minute** ([Buffer](https://buffer.com/resources/longer-tiktoks-get-more-views-data/)). A 60-second video that holds an average of 20 to 25 seconds is already far above the median, which makes "beat your own average watch time" a more defensible target than an unsourced percentage.

On hook structure, a vendor dataset of 34,635 clips found that hooks showing the result or object immediately averaged about twice the views of the worst-performing type, which was the **story-setup opener** ([OpusClip](https://www.opus.pro/blog/anatomy-of-a-viral-tiktok-2026)). TikTok's own advertising research points the same way: over 63% of the highest click-through ads state their key message in the first three seconds ([TikTok for Business](https://ads.tiktok.com/business/en-US/blog/9-creative-tips-to-drive-auction-ad-performance)). That is ad data, not organic data, and the OpusClip sample covers that vendor's users across TikTok and YouTube Shorts. For a history channel the implication is still clear: opening on chronology ("In 1954, on a remote atoll...") is the weakest available structure; opening on the detonation, the consequence or the striking claim is the strongest.

### Length is a weak lever for reach and a hard gate for money

Two large datasets disagree. Buffer's 1.1 million posts show median reach rising with length, from 194 for 5 to 10 second videos to 432.5 for videos over a minute ([Buffer](https://buffer.com/resources/longer-tiktoks-get-more-views-data/)). OpusClip's top decile is shorter than average, with a median of 41 seconds against 50 overall, and the 60 to 90 second bucket under-represented among viral clips ([OpusClip](https://www.opus.pro/blog/anatomy-of-a-viral-tiktok-2026)). The likeliest reconciliation is that longer videos are made disproportionately by more serious creators, while pure-reach outliers cluster at 30 to 60 seconds. The practical reading: a video padded to cross one minute with a dead final stretch probably loses to its 45-second version. Monetisation is unambiguous, though. Creator Rewards pays only on original videos over one minute ([Creator Academy](https://www.tiktok.com/creator-academy/en/article/monetization-creativity-program-video-eligible)).

### Captions, hashtags and sounds matter less than believed

Burned-in captions appear in **80.2% of top-decile clips** ([OpusClip](https://www.opus.pro/blog/anatomy-of-a-viral-tiktok-2026)), a correlation from a vendor sample. Hashtags give a small measured lift, nearly 5% more views for posts with at least one ([Metricool](https://metricool.com/press-release-tiktok-study-2026/)), and TikTok has capped them at five per post since mid-2025 with the in-app note "Use accurate hashtags to get more views" ([Social Media Today](https://www.socialmediatoday.com/news/tiktok-implements-five-hashtag-limit-per-post/757857/)). Evidence for #fyp is confined to small or pre-selected samples and is likely confounded. Keywords in captions, speech and on-screen text are officially encouraged for search, and "search value" feeds Creator Rewards pay ([Search Engine Journal](https://searchenginejournal.com/tiktoks-new-creator-rewards-program-prioritizes-search-value/511376)), but their larger role is probably classification: telling the system which interest cluster to test the video on. That is inference from the official signal list. Nothing credible was found on trending versus original sounds or on the low-volume trending sound trick.

### Niche consistency, series and comment replies have almost no evidence

No official statement, dataset or documented case study quantifies how many videos the system needs to "learn" an account, whether off-niche followers carry a reach penalty, or how a pivot compares with a fresh account. Niche consistency plausibly helps through an ordinary channel: consistent captions and narration mean the same interest cluster gets tested each time, and viewers who liked one video seed the next. Series, playlists, "part 2" structures, pinned videos and custom covers have no measured evidence at all.

On comments, the one TikTok-specific number is that posts containing a question receive **26% more comments** ([Metricool](https://metricool.com/press-release-tiktok-study-2026/)). Buffer found that replying to comments correlates with higher engagement on six platforms, but TikTok was not among them and Buffer cautions that causality is unproven ([Buffer](https://buffer.com/resources/replying-to-comments-boosts-engagement/)). Shares outnumber comments about five to one for 10K to 50K accounts (120 against 25 per post) and are the fastest-growing interaction ([Socialinsider](https://www.socialinsider.io/social-media-benchmarks/tiktok)), which makes "would someone send this to a friend" a better design target than "would someone comment".

### What suppresses reach is written down

The documented suppressors are eligibility rules, not hidden penalties. The Community Guidelines in force since 24 September 2026 make ineligible for the For You feed **"Reused or unoriginal content posted without creative edits, such as clips that show someone else's watermark or logo"** and "Low-quality or minimally edited content" ([Community Guidelines](https://www.tiktok.com/community-guidelines/en/integrity-authenticity)). Creator Academy defines unoriginal content to include material "largely repurposed from another source without adding any creative edits" and "content combined from multiple sources with little or no additional information and/or content value" ([Creator Academy](https://www.tiktok.com/creator-academy/en/article/tiktok-originality-policy)). The consequence escalates: "accounts that don't break the rules still post a lot of content that's ineligible for the FYF. In those cases, we may make the account and its content ineligible for the FYF and harder to find" ([Community Guidelines](https://www.tiktok.com/community-guidelines/en/accounts-features)). Image and carousel posts get about one fifth the views of video ([Metricool](https://metricool.com/press-release-tiktok-study-2026/)). By contrast, "inconsistent posting suppresses reach" has no support, and TikTok itself says low views do not prove a restriction ([Creator Academy](https://www.tiktok.com/creator-academy/en/article/video-not-recommended)).

## High-view creators run one of three operating models

Evidence on daily routines is the weakest part of this research. Search results are dominated by tool-vendor posts, the best first-person accounts date from 2022 and 2023, and no verified hour-by-hour time split exists for any named creator. What the attributable examples do show is that there is no single top-creator cadence. They cluster into three models, and per-video effort trades off roughly inversely with volume.

| Model | Example | Cadence | Effort per video |
|---|---|---|---|
| Craft | Jenny Hoyos, YouTube Shorts ([Tubefilter](https://www.tubefilter.com/?p=176194)) | One a week, same day and time | 10 to 20 hours |
| Craft, faceless history | "Time Traveller POV" AI history videos ([CO/AI](https://getcoai.com/?p=55436)) | Not stated | About 8 hours |
| Personality | Lydia Keating ([Techno Sapiens](https://technosapiens.substack.com/p/lydiakeating)) | About 3 a day | Under a minute to an hour |
| Volume clipping | "KickClipper" ([Ssemble](https://www.ssemble.com/blog/how-this-tiktok-clipper-made-300k-clipping-ishowspeed)) | About 900 clips a month | Near zero, tool-generated |

None of the named creators combines high per-video effort with several posts a day without a team. A twice-daily automated history channel is therefore running a volume cadence on a format whose documented successes follow the craft model, and that mismatch is worth keeping in view.

The best-documented habit is that selection outranks polish. Hoyos sits on ideas for long periods, studies competitor videos and comments before producing, and will change an entire video to get a stronger hook; her hooks run no more than three seconds, lean on concrete words, then immediately foreshadow the conflict and end on the strongest twist ([Marketing Examined](https://marketing-examined.beehiiv.com/p/jenny-hoyos-shortform-video-playbook)). Morning Brew Daily's social "clips" are not cut from the episode at all: they are rerecorded and scripted for social, and the team names topic selection as the critical variable ([Signal Awards](https://www.signalaward.com/2026/04/13/the-clip-economy-what-podcasters-are-getting-right-and-wrong/)). For a pipeline, that argues for spending the scarce human attention on topic and first three seconds and templating the rest.

On analytics, the metrics creators name are retention and the hook's viewed-versus-swiped rate; Hoyos targets 90% or better retention on Shorts ([Marketing Examined](https://marketing-examined.beehiiv.com/p/jenny-hoyos-shortform-video-playbook)). No first-person account of a structured TikTok post-mortem routine was found, and no evidence on when creators check numbers. On engagement, the single attributable habit is Hunter Prosper dedicating the first hour after posting to comments and direct messages ([Passionfruit](https://passionfru.it/hunter-prosper-tiktok-1014/)), from 2022; in the documented cases comments work more as an idea source and relationship habit than as a proven ranking lever.

The two formats closest to the reader's accounts have their own lessons. The faceless history channels with documented scale are AI-generated first-person "waking up in..." videos, one of which reached 53 million views, and historians have publicly criticised them for anachronisms and oversimplification ([CO/AI](https://getcoai.com/?p=55436)). No first-person process account from a narrated archive-footage channel was found, so that format has no proven template; accuracy is its available differentiator. Clipping, meanwhile, has industrialised into pay-per-view bounty networks, with one reported network of 40,000 freelance clippers and clips out-reaching their source shows by an order of magnitude ([Shira's newsletter](https://shiras-newsletter.beehiiv.com/p/we-re-all-living-in-the-clipping-economy)). The money in clipping comes from the source show or brand paying for reach, not from TikTok, and the rights-holder's participation is what makes the model work ([Loeb & Loeb](https://www.loeb.com/quicktakes/post/102lqhl/)).

Timelines are survivorship-biased and base rates are sobering: in a survey of more than 3,000 creators, half earned under $15,000 a year ([Influencer Marketing Hub](https://influencermarketinghub.com/creator-earnings-report-2025/)). One reported Creator Rewards rate was $1.20 per 1,000 qualified views for a 55,000-follower creator ([Simon Owens](https://simonowens.substack.com/p/has-tiktok-improved-its-revenue-share)).

## Policy risk separates the two accounts more than craft does

### AI narration is the smallest of the risks

The August 2026 Community Guidelines require a label only for AI or heavy editing that shows "realistic-looking scenes or people", and they state that disclosure is not needed when **"using generic text-to-speech (TTS) narration, when the TTS isn't a recognizable voice of a known individual"** ([Community Guidelines](https://www.tiktok.com/community-guidelines/en/integrity-authenticity)). A generic synthetic voice over genuine archive film therefore needs no AI label. A label becomes mandatory if the voice imitates a real person or if any shot is AI-generated or AI-altered to read as real footage. Over-labelling is not a safe default: TikTok calls mislabelling unaltered content a Terms of Service violation, labels cannot be removed after posting ([Help Center](https://support.tiktok.com/en/using-tiktok/creating-videos/ai-generated-content)), and labelled videos fall under the viewer-side AI slider. TikTok says the label "will not have an impact on organic engagement" ([Creator Academy](https://www.tiktok.com/creator-academy/en/article/ai-generated-content-label)). One mechanical hazard: TikTok auto-applies an irremovable label to uploads carrying C2PA Content Credentials, so a tool in the pipeline that writes that metadata can trigger it.

The softer risk is reputational and unquantified. A study reported in June 2026 classed 59% of videos recommended to new TikTok accounts as "AI slop" ([Tubefilter](https://www.tubefilter.com/?p=192734)), and July 2026 press reported TikTok testing filters against accounts posting low-quality AI video, though that coverage could not be opened and its scope is unverified ([Siècle Digital](https://siecledigital.fr/2026/07/15/tiktok-declare-la-guerre-aux-comptes-qui-saturent-la-plateforme-de-videos-generees-par-ia/)). No TikTok source defines "AI slop" as a policy category, and whether third-party TTS audio is detected or down-weighted is undocumented. No evidence compares human and AI voice performance.

### Reused footage is where the two channels diverge

TikTok never defines "meaningful transformation" and gives no threshold. Its operative words are "anything new", "creative edits", and "original commentary, original perspective, original editing", with the pointed question "am I analyzing here, or just repeating what someone else said?" ([Creator Academy](https://www.tiktok.com/creator-academy/en/article/high-quality-checklist-rewards)). The stricter test sits in the monetisation rules, which exclude "content reproduced from others with only slight modifications, including videos that are sped up or contain filters, fixed texts, or stickers" and content from other sources "without new and personal ideas" ([Help Center](https://support.tiktok.com/en/business-and-creator/creator-rewards-program/creator-rewards-program)).

The archive channel's original script, research, structure and motion graphics are the kind of addition those words describe, though no first-party example confirms that narration alone suffices. The podcast-clip channel's re-captioning and re-framing are what TikTok calls slight modifications. Both channels share the most explicit trigger: a visible third-party watermark or logo, which archive transfers and podcast video both commonly carry. The clip channel has a second exposure that is easy to miss. TikTok defines "cropping or cutting phrases to change meaning" and "rearranging or combining clips" as significant editing ([Community Guidelines](https://www.tiktok.com/community-guidelines/en/integrity-authenticity)), so tight cuts that alter what a named guest appears to say move a clip into the labelled or ineligible tiers.

### Copyright makes the clip channel's survival someone else's decision

TikTok issues a strike for each removal on a rightsholder's report, bans at three copyright strikes, and expires strikes after 90 days. Its list of appeal arguments that are "generally not accepted" includes "only a small portion of the work was copied" and "there are others who are also posting similar content" ([Help Center](https://support.tiktok.com/en/safety-hc/account-and-user-safety/copyright)). An unlicensed clip account therefore exists at the discretion of every show it clips. No TikTok enforcement data specific to podcast-clip accounts was found, and no court decision on short-form podcast clipping was located, so the size of this risk in practice is unknown; its shape is not.

The archive channel stands on firmer ground: "Copyright protection under this title is not available for any work of the United States Government" ([17 U.S.C. § 105](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/17/105)). The residual risks are specific. The Prelinger Archives warn "DO NOT ASSUME that all Prelinger Archives films are in the public domain", and that public-domain films "may occasionally incorporate copyrighted elements (such as music, images and archival footage)" ([Prelinger Archives](https://www.panix.com/~footage/prelarch.html)). Contractor-made films, third-party restorations and embedded music all need checking film by film.

### Automation is a grey zone with no detection evidence

The August 2026 guidelines state: **"We strictly prohibit automation tools, scripts, or other tricks designed to bypass our systems."** They define spam as "using automation to run many accounts or send repetitive content" and prohibit "using bots or scripts to write fake reviews or comments, or to increase likes or shares" ([Community Guidelines](https://www.tiktok.com/community-guidelines/en/integrity-authenticity)). Driving the official app on a real phone to post one's own videos is named in none of this text, but the first sentence is broad enough to be applied to it. The sanctioned route does not fit either: the Content Posting API keeps unaudited uploads private and lists as not acceptable "a utility tool to help upload contents to the account(s) you or your team manages" ([TikTok for Developers](https://developers.tiktok.com/doc/content-sharing-guidelines)). The only first-party automated option is TikTok Studio's web bulk upload and scheduling ([Creator Academy](https://www.tiktok.com/creator-academy/en/article/creator-rewards-program)).

Volume is not the issue. Two posts a day is far below the API's own ceiling of "typically around 15 posts per day" ([TikTok for Developers](https://developers.tiktok.com/doc/content-sharing-guidelines)). On detection, the honest position is ignorance: no TikTok documentation and no reliable third-party research covers detection of Android UI automation, and no verified case of an account banned for UI-automated posting of original content was found. Everything circulating comes from vendors of anti-detect tools. The documented consequence ladder, if TikTok does decide an account is deceptive, runs from verification challenges through posting restrictions and loss of For You eligibility to bans that extend to "associated accounts" ([Community Guidelines](https://www.tiktok.com/community-guidelines/en/accounts-features)).

AI-drafted, human-approved replies on the account's own videos are prohibited by no text found. The risk rises if replies go out without approval, read as near-identical, or post at machine speed. A cap of three replies per video on the two newest posts is conservative by any reading of the rules.

### Two accounts on one phone is allowed; the danger is linkage

"You can have multiple accounts... but not to deceive others or break the rules" ([Community Guidelines](https://www.tiktok.com/community-guidelines/en/integrity-authenticity)). No credible evidence shows that co-located accounts lose reach. What the rules do say is that serious violations can bring bans of "all of their accounts, including associated accounts". A copyright ban on the clip account could therefore take the history account with it. How TikTok decides accounts are associated is undisclosed.

### Sensitive content has clear red lines and one open question

Documentary and educational content is listed as allowed across the violence and weapons sections, and TikTok says captions and voiceover are how that context is established ([Community Guidelines](https://www.tiktok.com/community-guidelines/en/enforcement)). The outright prohibitions include real-world "dead bodies" and "charred, or burned bodies" ([Community Guidelines](https://www.tiktok.com/community-guidelines/en/sensitive-mature-themes)), which rules out Hiroshima and Nagasaki casualty footage and radiation-injury film regardless of historical value. The open question for the history channel is that "showing or promoting... firearms or explosive weapons" sits in a tier that is age-restricted and For You-ineligible ([Community Guidelines](https://www.tiktok.com/community-guidelines/en/regulated-commercial-activities)); no TikTok text says how historic detonation footage is treated, and a public-interest exception can keep a video up while still removing it from recommendations. Two framing risks follow from the misinformation section: undated archive footage can read as "unedited media that is presented out of context", and "what the government hid" framing edges toward the ineligible conspiracy tier when unsourced ([Community Guidelines](https://www.tiktok.com/community-guidelines/en/integrity-authenticity)). For the military channel the risks are glorifying violence, tactical instruction, firearms on screen, and b-roll of combat or casualties.

### Monetisation is gated by geography, length and originality

Creator Rewards requires a Personal account based and registered in the US, UK, Germany, Japan, South Korea, France, Mexico or Brazil, 10,000 followers and 100,000 views in the last 30 days ([Creator Academy](https://www.tiktok.com/creator-academy/en/article/eligibility)), and only views from those eight countries count ([Creator Academy](https://www.tiktok.com/creator-academy/en/article/monetization-creativity-program-qualified-view)). Videos must be over one minute and "filmed, designed, and produced entirely by yourself" ([Help Center](https://support.tiktok.com/en/business-and-creator/creator-rewards-program/creator-rewards-program)). On that text the clip channel is excluded. The history channel is not excluded by name, but its footage originates elsewhere, so acceptance hinges on reviewers crediting the script and edit. Reports from May 2026 of creators disqualified for originality despite filming everything themselves come from a low-authority outlet and are unverified ([Baller Alert](https://balleralert.com/tiktok-creator-rewards-disqualification/)), but they suggest false positives are a live possibility. A bio containing contact information is itself a listed ineligibility factor.

## Evidence-ranked takeaways for the two accounts

The ranking below orders actions by the strength of the evidence behind them, not by expected payoff. The top tier rests on TikTok's written rules; the middle on large observational datasets; the bottom on inference or practitioner habit.

| Rank | Action | Applies to | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Remove every third-party watermark, logo, slate and lower-third before posting | Both | TikTok's written policy, named example |
| 2 | Check per-video Analytics for ineligibility notices and run Account check at least every two weeks; appeal instead of deleting | Both | Written policy |
| 3 | Secure permission, join an official clipping campaign, or convert clips into narrated commentary | Clips | Written policy on originality and copyright |
| 4 | Isolate the clip account's risk from the history account | Both | Written policy on associated accounts |
| 5 | Keep generic TTS unlabelled; label only cloned voices or generated visuals; strip C2PA metadata risks | History | Written policy |
| 6 | Cut casualty, injury and animal-test footage; date and source footage on screen | History | Written policy |
| 7 | Keep twice-daily posting, judge it by the top decile, and expect a median near 500 views | Both | Large fixed-effects dataset |
| 8 | Open on the payoff shot or claim, never on chronology | Both | Vendor data plus official ad research |
| 9 | Burn in captions; use three to five accurate hashtags; end on a genuine question | Both | Vendor and tool datasets, small effects |
| 10 | Aim for a tight 40 to 60 seconds for reach; go past one minute only when the retention holds and monetisation is reachable | Both | Conflicting datasets |
| 11 | Diagnose the legacy-follower question with the follower versus non-follower split | History | Inference only |
| 12 | Keep human-approved comment replies; mine comments for topics | Both | No TikTok-specific evidence |

**The watermark and monitoring items come first because they are the only actions tied to explicit TikTok text.** A logo is the single named trigger for For You ineligibility. Account check scans only the last 30 posts, which at two posts a day is a 15-day window per account, so the pipeline needs a fortnightly check at minimum and should treat per-video Analytics notices as the durable record ([Creator Academy](https://www.tiktok.com/creator-academy/en/article/account-check)). Deleting does not help: "Deleting your violative content does not remove the violation" ([Help Center](https://support.tiktok.com/en/safety-hc/account-and-user-safety/content-violations-and-bans)), so any delete-and-repost reflex in automation should be removed. Because repeated ineligible posts can make a whole account ineligible, a twice-daily cadence of borderline content compounds the pattern quickly; when an ineligibility notice appears, the right response is to pause and fix the format, not to keep publishing.

**The podcast-clip channel needs a decision, not an optimisation.** As it stands it is unoriginal on TikTok's written definition, barred from Creator Rewards on the written text, and exposed to a three-strike ban it cannot control. The three compliant routes are written permission from each show, official clipping campaigns where the rights-holder supplies assets and pays per view, or genuine commentary in which the operator's own narration and analysis occupy a substantial share of the runtime. The Morning Brew approach of scripting purpose-built social segments is the documented model that fits originality rules. Until one of those is in place, the clip account should not share a device, login identity or content with the history account, on the understanding that separating accounts to evade an active restriction would itself be a violation. Clips must stay complete ideas that do not change what a guest said.

**The history channel should invest in what TikTok's wording rewards.** Its original script is its originality claim, so the script should analyse and interpret, not narrate a montage; compilations with "little or no additional information" are the named failure case. It should keep a per-film provenance log (source URL, producing agency, rights statement) plus script drafts and project files, because those substitute for the production photos TikTok asks for in appeals. Stills-heavy edits sit close to the excluded "slide videos" category and are riskier than moving footage. Music should be absent, from TikTok's library, or licensed and under a minute. Whether detonation footage is caught by the "explosive weapons" tier is unknown; the Analytics ineligibility notice is the only way to find out, and the result is worth tracking per video.

**On craft, the best-supported changes are cheap.** Lead with the blast, the consequence or the claim. Compare each video's average watch time with the account's own trailing average and the 11.3-second platform median, not with an invented threshold. Use the question at the end as a real question, since like-for-like tricks are ineligible. Posting time deserves no engineering effort beyond following the account's own follower-activity chart.

**The legacy-follower question can be tested instead of debated.** If the 11,000 followers from the unrelated viral clip are dragging the new niche, the mechanism would be followers swiping away early. For each new-niche video, TikTok Studio shows the follower versus non-follower viewer split and average watch time. If non-followers already dominate the audience and views are still low, the problem is cold-audience retention and a fresh account would not fix it. If followers dominate and watch poorly, the legacy audience is the drag and starting a clean account becomes rational. No published evidence settles which case is typical; the platform-wide traffic split, with the Following feed at about 0.3% of views, suggests followers are a small share of impressions for videos that get distributed at all, but says nothing about the first few hundred.

**On automation, the lowest-risk design keeps the automation narrow.** Posting the operator's own videos at a human-plausible cadence, with no automated likes, follows or unsolicited comments, stays clear of every named trigger. TikTok Studio's web scheduler is the only route TikTok itself offers, and its web-only "Content check lite" pre-screens a video against For You eligibility standards before posting ([Creator Academy](https://www.tiktok.com/creator-academy/en/article/content-check-lite)), which the phone path cannot reach. The comment-reply design is already conservative and should stay human-approved and varied.

## Conclusion

The folklore about TikTok concentrates on the part of the system that is unknowable and mostly outside a creator's control: batch sizes, thresholds, hidden penalties. The documented part is duller and more useful. Ranking is per viewer and per video, driven by predicted watch time, and eligibility is governed by written rules that TikTok exposes through in-app notices. For an automated pipeline that changes what to instrument. The valuable feedback loop is not views by posting hour; it is average watch time against the account's own baseline, the follower versus non-follower split, and the ineligibility notices that say whether a video was allowed to compete at all.

The two accounts are not variations of one strategy. The history channel is a slow craft bet whose asset is original scripting over free footage, running at a volume cadence that the evidence says is harmless to per-post reach but unproven for quality. The clip channel is a distribution service without a client: clipping pays when the source show pays for it, and survives when the source show permits it. The questions this research could not answer are the ones a pipeline is well placed to answer for itself, because it produces 28 controlled observations a week: whether a second daily post cannibalises the first, whether detonation footage trips the weapons tier, whether legacy followers suppress early retention, and whether replies move anything. Published evidence on each of those is absent, and the accounts' own analytics are now the best available source.
